2. Main Discussion: Should AI Be Publicly Owned?

Background

Recent proposals internationally have suggested that governments could acquire equity stakes in major AI companies. Advocates argue this would allow the public to benefit financially from technologies built upon publicly funded research and societal knowledge.

Participants explored several different interpretations of "public ownership," including:

  • Government ownership of AI companies.
  • Public ownership of AI infrastructure and data centers.
  • Public ownership of models themselves.
  • Open-source and publicly funded AI alternatives.
  • National AI sovereignty initiatives.

Key Discussion Themes

1. Government Ownership vs. Regulation

Participants questioned whether governments should become shareholders in AI companies.

Concerns Raised

  • Potential conflicts of interest if governments act simultaneously as investors and regulators.
  • Lack of clarity regarding what influence share ownership would actually provide.
  • Risks that governments could claim credit for AI successes while distancing themselves from failures.
  • Questions regarding transparency and accountability in public investments.

Potential Benefits

  • Public participation in the economic gains generated by AI.
  • Ability to fund public services or social programs from AI-related revenues.
  • Greater influence over strategic AI development.

2. Who Owns AI-Generated Value?

A significant portion of the discussion focused on ownership of the data and knowledge underlying AI systems.

Participants noted that:

  • AI models are trained on large collections of human-generated knowledge and content.
  • Much of this content originates from individuals, communities, and creators who are not compensated.
  • Questions remain about whether AI-generated wealth should primarily benefit corporations, governments, or the broader public.

Some participants suggested that ownership debates should begin with data, knowledge, and infrastructure rather than corporate equity.

3. Public Trust and Control

The group explored whether publicly owned AI systems would be more trustworthy than corporate systems.

Several perspectives emerged:

  • Some participants indicated greater trust in publicly owned systems because they may be subject to democratic oversight.
  • Others argued ownership alone does not guarantee accountability, transparency, or improved governance.
  • Concerns were raised about who would determine the values embedded within publicly controlled AI systems.
  • Participants noted that public ownership might simply shift control from corporations to governments without resolving deeper governance issues.

4. AI Sovereignty

The discussion examined efforts by countries to establish sovereign AI capabilities.

Topics included:

  • National investments in domestic AI companies.
  • Canadian efforts to support AI firms and promote AI sovereignty.
  • European development of publicly supported open-source AI models.
  • The strategic importance of maintaining national AI capacity.

Participants questioned what sovereignty actually means in practice, noting that ownership, infrastructure, investment, governance, and data location all contribute to sovereign capability.

Some argued that maintaining headquarters within a country is not sufficient if infrastructure, funding, or operational control remain international.

5. Infrastructure and Data Centers

Participants emphasized that AI governance depends heavily on physical infrastructure.

Issues discussed included:

  • Ownership and location of data centers.
  • Dependence on foreign cloud providers.
  • Security and cybersecurity concerns.
  • Replication, resiliency, and national control of critical AI infrastructure.
  • Environmental impacts associated with large-scale AI deployment.
